\section{Simulated Epistemic Shift -- Sequence Evolution}
\label{sec:epistemic-shift-alisim}

Instead of shuffled/random DNA as OOD inputs, I simulated real sequences
evolving away from themselves. I took 400 real \texttt{promoter\_all} test
sequences (200 pos/200 neg) and evolved each one under an HKY85+$\Gamma_4$
model ($\kappa{=}2$, $\alpha{=}1$, empirical base frequencies) using
AliSim~\citep{ly2022alisim}, fixing the real sequence as the simulation root
so it's a genuine perturbation, not a random draw. Each anchor was evolved
at 19 branch lengths (0 to 12.0 substitutions/site -- extended past an
initial 0-3.0 grid once I found identity to anchor hadn't reached the
model's own saturation floor by t=3.0), 7,600 sequences total. I don't
assume the label still holds at high divergence -- accuracy is tracked, not
treated as ground truth there. \textbf{Scope}: DNABERT2 only, single
checkpoint/seed (seed 1) except the two deep ensembles (5 and 3 independently
fine-tuned checkpoints respectively); not checked against the other three
backbones or additional seeds.

\section{Simulated Aleatoric Shift -- Decision-Boundary Sampling}
\label{sec:aleatoric-shift-boundary}

Instead of flipping labels at random, I used the base model's own confidence
to find genuinely ambiguous examples -- no synthetic corruption at all. For
each real \texttt{promoter\_all} test sequence ($n{=}1{,}584$) I computed a
margin score $m=|2p_1-1|$ and split into quartiles ($Q_1$ = near the
boundary, $Q_4$ = confident). \textbf{Scope}: same as above -- DNABERT2
only, single checkpoint/seed except the ensembles.

\section{Uncertainty Decomposition}
\label{sec:discussion-decomposition}

The short version: on both axes, and for every method tested -- including
true independently-trained deep ensembles, the case the formal decomposition
is supposed to hold up best for -- epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty do
not move independently of each other the way a clean decomposition would
require. \texttt{rf} is a genuine, partial exception on the epistemic axis
specifically (see below), not evidence the decomposition works in general.

Each dataset isolates one theorized cause of uncertainty while holding the
other fixed. The boundary-sampling dataset keeps the input distribution
completely fixed -- every example is a real, in-distribution
\texttt{promoter\_all} sequence -- and only varies how ambiguous the true
label is. A correct decomposition should show $U_{\text{aleatoric}}$ rising
while $U_{\text{epistemic}}$ stays flat. The sequence-evolution dataset does
the reverse: every anchor starts as a confidently-labeled real sequence, and
only the input's distance from the training distribution is varied, with
label difficulty never entering the picture. A correct decomposition should
show $U_{\text{epistemic}}$ rising while $U_{\text{aleatoric}}$ stays flat.

On the epistemic-shift axis, epistemic barely moves for most methods and
several (\texttt{conv\_epinet}, \texttt{laplace} -- not shown in the trimmed
table above, see \texttt{.docs/COMPLETE\_RESULT\_TABLES.md}) peak partway
through the grid and decline rather than keep rising -- only \texttt{rf}
shows a real, still-strengthening, non-reversing rise all the way to the
extended endpoint. On the aleatoric-shift axis, aleatoric rises exactly as
it should for every method, but epistemic rises right along with it for
every method too, even though nothing about these examples is
out-of-distribution -- they're just hard, in-distribution examples.
\texttt{rf} leaks the least there too, but still leaks (epistemic
$\rho{=}-0.443$, not $\approx 0$) -- it is the best available method on both
axes among those tested here, not a method that solves the decomposition
problem.

\textbf{Relation to prior in-repo findings}: this reinforces, rather than
reverses, the qualified verdict in
\texttt{.docs/ALEATORIC\_EPISTEMIC\_CROSSCHECK.md}, which already found
(on a separate, label-noise-based axis) that the confound narrows for
independently-sampled methods on one axis but not the other. The boundary
dataset's result here -- \texttt{rf}/\texttt{laplace} leaking least, the
independently-trained ensembles leaking among the most -- is consistent
with, not contradictory to, that earlier, more qualified conclusion.
